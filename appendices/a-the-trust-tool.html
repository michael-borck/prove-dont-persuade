A. The Trust Tool

The trust tool is the one idea this book uses in every chapter. It is small enough to fit on a sticky note and general enough to survive any model swap. Before you act on any AI output, you place it on a grid by answering two questions, and the grid tells you what to do next: skim it, check the load-bearing bit, or send it through a human before it touches anything real.

Almost every public AI failure is a placement failure. The chatbot that gave wrong legal advice, the hallucinated citation in a filed court document, the churn flag nobody checked: each is someone treating a red-quadrant output as green. The model rarely fails uniquely. The placement fails, because nobody made it a conscious act. This appendix is the tool in full.

The two questions

  1. Does this output need to be precise, or is average fine?
    • Average means roughly right is good enough: a brainstorm, a first draft, a summary that points you where to look. If it is a little off, the cost is small.
    • Precise means the answer has to be right, because someone or something will act on it: a customer gets called, a contract clause gets relied on, a fault gets cleared.
  2. If it is wrong, how big is the blast radius, small or large?
    • Small means a mistake costs a glance or a re-do: a misrouted ticket, a clumsy sentence.
    • Large means a mistake lands on a real person, a real budget, or a real decision: a churn prediction, a hiring screen, a support reply a customer reads.

Answer the two and you have a coordinate on the grid.

The grid

Small blast radius Large blast radius
Average is fine 🟢 green 🟡 yellow
Must be precise 🟡 yellow 🔴 red

Two of the four cells collapse to the same action, which is why the tool reduces to a traffic light.

What each placement tells you to do

  • 🟢 Green, skim and use. The output is roughly right and the cost of being wrong is small. Read it, use it, move on. Drafts, brainstorms, document pulls, triage of low-stakes tickets.
  • 🟡 Yellow, verify the load-bearing bit. Most of the output can stand, but one claim carries the weight, and that is the claim you check. A strategic framing, a retrieved answer with a source, a summary that feeds a decision. Find the sentence a decision would rest on, and confirm it.
  • 🔴 Red, a human signs off before it touches anything real. The output must be right and a mistake lands on a person. A churn flag acted on, a customer reply sent, a fault cleared, a hire screened. Human-in-the-loop, non-negotiable, and the model said so is never a defence.

How to place well

The tool is a discipline, not a formula. These are the moves the chapters rehearse.

Place before you act. Placement is a conscious step before you ship, because the surface will never warn you. A fluent, polished output is exactly the one nobody checks, and that is the danger.

Judge outputs, not models. A frontier model and a local four-bit Qwen can each produce green-quadrant brilliance and red-quadrant garbage on the same afternoon. You are learning to judge the output in front of you, not the brand on the box. Your judgement has to survive a model swap, because the swap is coming.

The same screen can hold two placements. One output can flip quadrant mid-sentence. A brainstorm of candidate churn drivers is green; the instant a specific customer count and revenue figure appears, the same screen is red. A segment description (“Basic-tier customers churn at roughly three times the base rate”) is green; a per-customer score (“flag customer C0xxx for a save call”) is red. Read each claim, not each screen.

Fluency raises the stakes. A confident, well-structured output disarms scrutiny. The better the prose, the less likely anyone checks, so the more disciplined your placement has to be. Confidence is a delivery property, not a truth property.

Anything that touches a person is red until a human has checked it. A churn flag, a customer persona, a screening ranking, a support reply: each lands on a real person, so each is red regardless of how polished it looks. This is the rule that runs under every chapter.

Place per step, not per workflow. The moment you give a model tools and let it act (an agent), placement becomes a decision per step. Clustering might be yellow, retrieval green, the final brief red. Put your checkpoint just before the step that becomes load-bearing or irreversible, and be able to defend why there, and not one step later.

Controls move red toward yellow; they do not retire your judgement. Good architecture, privacy-by-design, a sourced retrieval step: these shrink the blast radius or make the claim checkable. They move a red output to yellow (“verify the load-bearing bits before sending”). They do not promote it to green. A human still reads it.

Re-place as capability shifts. A model that scores 0% on faults this year might creep to 99% next year, and a red square could move to yellow. But only a fresh evaluation moves it, not hope, and not the vendor’s demo. Re-place on evidence, on a schedule.

When yellow or red: the four lenses

When a placement tells you to verify, Chapter 3’s four lenses turn “check it” into something you can actually do. For each lens, write one sentence of evidence pulled from the actual output, not a vibe.

  • Representativeness: who is in the data or output? Who is missing or thinly drawn?
  • Harm: if this output drove a decision, who could be disadvantaged, and would they know?
  • Accountability: who is responsible if it is wrong? Can they be identified and reached?
  • Transparency: can you see how it produced the answer, and show someone else?

Representativeness and Harm tell you the blast radius. Accountability and Transparency tell you whether a human is genuinely in the loop. Fail either pair and the output stays red until it is fixed, not shipped. “It seems a bit biased” is not evidence; “the at-risk profile defaults to one industry and company size, which excludes every customer in the South and Metro territories” is.

Worked placements across Tessera

The chapters run the same tool over and over. A representative sweep:

Output Placement Why
Brainstorm of candidate churn drivers 🟢 green average, small blast; scopes the problem
Segment description, “dissatisfied customers churn well above the base rate” 🟢 green average, nobody gets touched
Routing a healthy or degraded ticket 🟢 green a wrong route costs a human a glance
Retrieved RAG answer (with a source to quote) 🟡 yellow verify the load-bearing citation
Sentiment gap feeding a product decision 🟡 yellow → red verify against the raw tickets
“The model works across all our regions” 🟡 yellow plausible prose over an unverified claim
Per-customer churn score, “flag C0xxx” 🔴 red precise, lands on a named person
Customer persona or screening ranking 🔴 red touches a person, drives a decision
Router fault confirmation (under dim light) 🔴 red a missed fault leaves a customer offline
Support reply a customer reads 🔴 red a human signs off before it sends

Notice how often the same analysis produces a green output and a red one a line apart. That is the discipline: read each claim, place each step.

The two mistakes to avoid

Treating red as green is the failure mode this book exists to catch. It is rarely malicious; it is the natural drift of a polished output nobody made themselves place.

Blobbing a workflow into one cell is the agent-era version of the same mistake. An agent is a chain of outputs, each with its own failure surface. One placement for the whole chain means the red step slips through unnamed. Place per step; checkpoint the red ones.
